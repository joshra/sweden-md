# Hilma af Klint: When Art History Took Decades to See Her

> **30-second overview:** Hilma af Klint (1862–1944) began making radically nonobjective paintings in 1906, before several men later placed at the center of modern abstraction. Her work remained outside public view for decades. Seeing it now is not simply about restoring a “forgotten woman,” but asking who gets to become an origin point in art history.

## Why it matters

Hilma af Klint is often introduced with an irresistible claim: she was the first abstract artist. The claim can correct an old exclusion, but it can also flatten a difficult life into a new winner’s podium. Her deeper importance is that her work reveals how abstraction did not emerge along a single road led by manifesto-writing men in Paris or Munich.

The familiar modern-art story taught in Sweden, too, long followed that canonical route. Af Klint’s monumental paintings opened another possibility: a formally trained woman who earned a living through portraits and landscapes could develop a rigorous abstract language between spiritual practice, close attention to nature, and contemporary scientific imagination. She is not merely an isolated-genius tale. Her work is a fault line running through art history’s established categories.

## A woman artist able to work within the system

Born near Stockholm in 1862, af Klint studied at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts from 1882 to 1888. She won an Academy prize for figure painting and showed conventional portraits, botanical studies, and landscapes in more than twenty exhibitions. Her later abstraction did not emerge from an inability to represent the visible world; it was a deliberate turn away from skills she had already mastered.

Her education also complicates a simple exclusion story. Women could enter parts of the Nordic art establishment in the late nineteenth century, yet access to an academy did not guarantee equal collecting, exhibition, or historical recognition. Af Klint’s public career and private experiments coexisted: one allowed her to work in her own society, while the other produced the legacy that later transformed her reputation.

## The Five and images of an invisible world

In 1896, af Klint formed the spiritualist circle The Five (De Fem) with Anna Cassel, Cornelia Cederberg, Sigrid Hedman, and Mathilda Nilsson. During their meetings, the women practiced automatic writing and drawing and recorded the results in notebooks. To dismiss this as eccentric biography would miss its period context. Across Europe and the United States, intellectual and artistic circles were trying to reconcile religion and spiritualism with new science and ideas about invisible natural forces.

From these practices, af Klint developed a vocabulary of circles, spirals, ovals, letters, flowers, and cell-like forms. At times they resemble cosmological charts; at others, biological structures not yet named. The images do more than simplify visible objects. They attempt to picture relationships beyond ordinary sight: matter and spirit, male and female, microscopic life and the vast cosmos, opposed yet interpenetrating.

## The Paintings for the Temple: more than one road to abstraction

Between 1906 and 1915, af Klint completed the vast project known as The Paintings for the Temple. Within it, The Ten Largest uses sheets nearly three metres high to address childhood, youth, adulthood, and old age. Bright fields of colour, petals, spirals, and writing form a system that feels playful and ceremonial at once. She imagined the works installed in a spiral temple, though the building was never realized.

Her nonobjective work from 1906 did precede some famous breakthroughs by Kandinsky, Malevich, and Mondrian. Still, naming her the one definitive “first” is too neat. Abstraction was not a race with an agreed finish line; artists differed in what they considered abstract, what they exhibited publicly, and what they argued in theory. Af Klint’s more consequential revision is to show that modernism developed through more dispersed routes than textbooks once allowed—routes shaped by women’s collaboration, spiritual experimentation, and knowledge of nature.

## Delayed viewing and the rewriting of the present

Af Klint rarely exhibited these nonobjective works and marked some notebooks to remain unopened until twenty years after her death. When she died in 1944, her nephew Erik af Klint inherited about 1,300 paintings, 124 notebooks, and more than 26,000 written pages. A 1986 group exhibition at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art became a decisive moment in her international reception. The Guggenheim Museum’s 2018–19 retrospective then made her a major public route into reconsidering abstraction.

Yet “discovery” is not the end of the story. Research must still examine her relationships with women collaborators, what spiritual guidance meant within her practice, and whether the desire for a pioneering woman has made later audiences tidy away the work’s resistant parts. The conservation and digitization undertaken through Moderna Museet and the Hilma af Klint Foundation allow that rewriting to rest on primary material rather than a replacement legend.

For Taiwanese readers, her story is also a useful lesson in museum literacy. Collections and canons do not form naturally. What is preserved, studied, exhibited, and translated shapes what later generations believe art was able to become. Af Klint’s return tells us that adding a name matters—but changing the habits of looking that once failed to see her matters more.
