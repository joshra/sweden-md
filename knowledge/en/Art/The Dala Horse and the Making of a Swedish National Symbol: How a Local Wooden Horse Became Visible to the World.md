# The Dala Horse and the Making of a Swedish National Symbol: How a Local Wooden Horse Became Visible to the World

> **30-second overview:** Today's Dala horse can look like an ancient Swedish symbol handed down in one fixed form. In fact, regional woodworking and painting, household production, craft retail, product standardisation, and world's fairs gradually made it recognisable. It reminds us that national images are rarely discovered; they are made, selected, sold, and repeatedly displayed.

## Why it matters

In Taiwan, the Dala horse tends to appear in Nordic design shops, at Swedish events, or on souvenir shelves: a red body covered in flowing floral lines, immediately legible as Sweden. That success can make it seem as though the horse has always been an ancient, fixed emblem shared by the whole country.

Since moving from Sweden to Taiwan, I have often been asked what object is most typically Swedish. The Dala horse is a good answer, but it also invites a better question: why was this particular horse chosen? The answer lies not only in folklore but in labour, distribution, design, and international exhibitions. Its history shows how a local object becomes a national image, and how “tradition” can preserve difference while also arranging it into an easily recognised form.

## Before the souvenir, there was no single purpose

Dalarnas museum dates the oldest known surviving wooden horse from the region to the sixteenth century. Found in a chest in Malung together with a forked object, it may have been a toy or may have carried ritual or magical meaning; the evidence does not settle the question. That uncertainty matters because it prevents a neat origin story from replacing a complicated history.

The oldest known surviving painted horse, from around 1820, carries decoration connected to furniture painting by guild craftspeople in Falun. The horse was therefore not born in isolation as a national totem. It emerged where wood, tools, domestic painting traditions, and local life met. A horse could be a child's toy, a display of skill, an object of exchange, or a commodity without those identities cancelling one another.

## Household work and markets turned many horses into a product

Records from the mid-nineteenth century place wooden horses at markets in Dalarna and neighbouring Halsingland. Itinerant workers may also have exchanged them for food and lodging. A 1904 survey of Mora crafts described painted horses as both household supplementary work and products that could be sold across Sweden. Folk craft here belonged to a real economy: carving and painting were not only performances of tradition, but part of household income.

In 1906, a Mora craft organisation opened a shop and made Dala horses an important product. Stable distribution created stable demand, encouraging makers and retailers to regularise size, silhouette, and decoration. The familiar red painted horse was not the only ancient prototype. It became highly recognisable as varied local forms were gradually organised for a market.

Standardisation worked in two directions. It reduced some earlier variation, but also gave makers access to a larger market and allowed the horse to remain recognisable far from home. National symbols often gain power through this trade-off: they must be simplified to travel, yet successful simplification can erase the memory that many versions came before.

## World's fairs enlarged a local object into Sweden

According to Dalarnas museum, the Dala horse became a popular souvenir in the Swedish pavilion at the 1937 Paris world's fair. At the 1939 New York World's Fair, a large red painted horse stood at the pavilion entrance. Its enlarged scale also enlarged its meaning: before visitors entered, a regional wooden horse was already teaching them how to recognise Sweden.

A world's fair is not a neutral display case. Countries have limited space in which to compress complex societies into images that foreign audiences can grasp quickly. The Dala horse's clear silhouette, crafted surface, and vivid colour could communicate both regional character and the cultural confidence of a modern state. The fair did not invent the horse in 1939; it was a crucial moment when international display translated a local souvenir into a national symbol.

The process resembles branding today. An object is photographed, enlarged, and reproduced, then returns home as part of a shared self-image. “Representing Sweden” was not an innate property of the horse. It was the accumulated effect of exhibitions, commerce, and countless later uses.

## A fixed symbol can still be repainted

Nordiska museet holds a horse made in Nusnas and acquired in 1937, decorated on a red ground with yellow, blue, black, and white `krusning` curves. Its museum record still identifies its object type as a toy. The detail is useful: even after becoming a national emblem, the horse retained overlapping identities as toy, craft object, souvenir, and collection piece.

Dalarnas museum likewise exhibits early horses beside contemporary free interpretations. Cultural heritage is therefore not simply the preservation of one correct silhouette. In the Swedish National Heritage Board's definition, it also includes skills, activities, traditions, and knowledge. What sustains the Dala horse is an entire relationship of carving, painting, selling, collecting, and redesigning.

For readers in Taiwan, the most revealing point may not be that Swedes like horses. It is how a simple national image conceals local work. The next time we see the red horse, we can recognise both its charm and the choices behind it: whose craft was enlarged, which differences were compressed, and whether a shared image can still make room for new versions.
