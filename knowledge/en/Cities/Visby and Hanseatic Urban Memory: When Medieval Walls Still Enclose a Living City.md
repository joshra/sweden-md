# Visby and Hanseatic Urban Memory: When Medieval Walls Still Enclose a Living City

> **30-second overview:** Visby, on the Baltic island of Gotland, was an important medieval Hanseatic trading town. Its surviving wall, streets, warehouses, and church ruins are striking, but its deeper importance lies in the fact that this history was never sealed inside a museum. Residents, shops, schools, visitors, and public events still negotiate what it means to preserve and inhabit the same city.

## Why it matters

Seen only in summer, Visby can look almost too complete: limestone walls, roses, narrow lanes, the sea, and crowds on holiday. But as a Swede living in Taiwan, the Visby I want to introduce to Taiwanese readers is not merely Sweden's most visibly historic city. It poses a harder question. When an old town still contains homes, businesses, and buildings that need repair, how can it retain its history without becoming a stage legible only to visitors?

Visby also belongs in Sweden.md because it puts Sweden back into the Baltic Sea. Sweden is often explained through Stockholm, the welfare state, or northern forests. Yet medieval sea routes, ports, merchants, and inter-urban rules had already connected Gotland to northern Europe and the continent. Visby's form reminds us that Sweden has never been only a country extending inland. It is also a country shaped by exchange across water.

## A city that grew from its harbour

Visby lies on Gotland, roughly one hundred kilometres east of the Swedish mainland. A natural harbour supported Baltic trade here from the Viking Age. By the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Gotlandic merchants and later German-speaking settlers had turned a seasonal trading place into an international commercial town. The harbour was not only where goods arrived. Languages, legal relationships, faiths, and urban building forms came ashore as well.

For readers familiar with Taiwan's port cities, Visby has a recognisable logic. Its identity was built less by an administrative boundary than by routes and exchange. The network here, however, was not made by steamships or container traffic but by medieval vessels, warehouses, and merchant alliances. The Hanseatic League was not a modern state, yet trade relations and shared interests among towns made Visby a node in a cross-regional order.

## A wall that protected and divided

Visby's clearest outline is its limestone town wall, begun in the thirteenth century. Today the towers and gates look romantic, but the wall was first an instrument of defence and power. It protected a wealthy commercial town while sharpening the distinction between inside and outside. By the late thirteenth century, tensions between urban citizens and rural Gotlanders even developed into open conflict. The wall therefore records not only security, but also the distance growing between a trading city and its hinterland.

Inside, another commercial map remains readable. Streets leading toward the harbour, medieval stone warehouses and merchants' houses, and church ruins all testify to the concentration of goods, wealth, and communities. UNESCO's emphasis is likewise not on one isolated monument. It is on the way the wall, street pattern, buildings, and later layers of life can still be understood together. What Visby preserves is a whole urban relationship, not simply an attractive old building.

## Decline left layers of time behind

Visby gradually lost its leading place in Baltic trade during the fourteenth century. Plague, the Danish invasion of 1361, later warfare, and changing trade routes all weakened the town. There is no neat curve from golden age to revival. The city stagnated, buildings acquired new uses, many churches were abandoned after the Reformation, and homes and public institutions accumulated inside the inherited plan.

Yet part of Visby's present value comes from the fact that it was never flattened by one comprehensive modernisation. Eighteenth-century housing and nineteenth-century schools and commerce did not fully replace the medieval city. They continued to grow within its scale. Preservation here is therefore not about freezing a single year. It means recognising that cities are made by several periods at once. When Visby entered UNESCO's World Heritage List in 1995, this continuing medieval form and layered urban life were central to its significance.

## The difficult everyday work of living heritage

I find UNESCO's description of Visby as an inhabited historic town especially useful because it pulls attention away from the photograph and back toward daily life. Homes, retail, culture, and tourism remain inside the World Heritage property. The wall and ruins require specialist care, while individual choices about windows, roofs, materials, and renovations can accumulate into changes in the whole townscape. Preservation is therefore not a command issued by one heritage agency. It is long-term work shared among the municipality, state institutions, the museum, residents, businesses, and property owners.

The balance is not romantic. Summer tourism brings jobs and resources for care, but can make the centre increasingly seasonal. Historic properties attract investment, but may reduce the diversity of permanent life. Using church ruins for events requires assessment of what those fragile places can carry. This is where Visby becomes contemporary: a town cannot survive on being well preserved alone, but neither can it consume the layers that make it distinct simply in order to remain active.

Looking at Visby from Taiwan, I do not see a European model to copy. I see the same question many historic districts face: preserved for whom? If the answer is only tourists, a city slowly becomes scenery. If preservation is ignored, local memory disappears through one individually reasonable alteration after another. Visby offers no perfect solution. It offers a Swedish practice of sustained negotiation, keeping history in the city while keeping real life there too.
