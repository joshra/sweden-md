# Sweden's Transition Organisations and Mobility Security: Connecting Job Loss to the Next Step

> **30-second overview:** In Sweden, job security does not only mean avoiding dismissal. Transition organisations created through collective agreements can provide career advice, job-search help, training, and financial support before and after redundancy. Since 2022, workers outside collective-agreement coverage have also had access to a basic public service. The aim is not to prevent economic change, but to give people a fairer chance to move through it.

## Why it matters

When I discuss Swedish working life in Taiwan, I often encounter an understandable assumption: a welfare state creates security by keeping a job from disappearing. The more revealing part of the Swedish model is that it also tries to attach security to the person. Firms still restructure and technology still displaces tasks. The institutional question is who provides the time, knowledge, and resources needed to reach the next job.

The Swedish word `omställning` suggests transition or readjustment. Here it is not a slogan telling individuals to become more adaptable. It describes an architecture built from collective agreements, employer financing, professional guidance, and public rules. Swedish economic flexibility therefore depends not only on firms' ability to change, but on workers' capacity to survive that change.

## Security attached to people, not only positions

Traditional employment protection focuses on dismissal rules and seniority. Transition support asks a different question: if a position cannot remain, can the worker's skills, income, and route to another job be addressed early? Services may include individual counselling, skills assessment, job-search assistance, short courses, business-start support, or planning for longer education.

This is not the same as unemployment insurance. The `a-kassa` system primarily replaces part of a person's income during unemployment; transition organisations focus on shortening unemployment, changing careers, and developing skills. The systems may connect, but they have different eligibility rules, funding streams, and functions. Calling all of them simply a welfare safety net hides how the pieces actually work.

## Collective agreements turn restructuring into shared infrastructure

Sweden has several transition organisations serving different agreement areas. In the private sector, for example, TRR serves covered salaried employees, while TSL serves manual and skilled workers within the Confederation of Swedish Enterprise–LO agreement area. They are neither ordinary public employment offices nor consultants hired ad hoc by a single company. They are standing institutions created by labour-market parties through collective agreements.

This arrangement captures a central feature of Swedish labour-market governance. Employers gain room to adjust staffing, but adjustment costs are not treated as the dismissed worker's private problem. Unions, meanwhile, pursue security not only by defending every existing position. Employer contributions and negotiated rules fund a capacity that an entire agreement area can use repeatedly.

## The 2022 reform extended a basic floor beyond collective agreements

Strong collective bargaining coverage does not mean every worker stands under the same umbrella. Sweden's 2022 Act on Basic Transition and Skills Support created a public service administered through Kammarkollegiet for eligible workers who lack collective-agreement support. Its basic offer includes counselling for dismissed workers, guidance for employed workers, and enhanced help for dismissed people with particular needs arising from illness.

The same reform package placed mid-career learning closer to the centre of employment security. Eligible workers can apply to CSN for `omställningsstudiestöd` to pursue education expected to strengthen their future position in the labour market. In 2026, its grant component is generally calculated at 80 per cent of previous income up to a weekly ceiling, normally for no more than 44 weeks of full-time study. Transition organisations can help shape a study plan, and some agreements add supplementary support, but eligibility and payment still require separate assessment.

## Mobility security still has boundaries

It is tempting to turn this system into a comforting story that Swedes need not fear unemployment. In practice, the relevant organisation and available support depend on the agreement area, employment history, reason for leaving, and application timing. The public service narrowed a coverage gap without making everyone's conditions identical. Study support is also constrained by eligibility tests, administration, and available funding.

Nor can counselling manufacture local vacancies or erase inequalities linked to age, health, language, or regional industrial decline. For Taiwanese readers, the useful lesson is therefore not a policy package ready for import. It is a sharper question: when an economy continually asks people to adapt, how should the cost of that adaptation be divided among individuals, employers, and shared institutions?
