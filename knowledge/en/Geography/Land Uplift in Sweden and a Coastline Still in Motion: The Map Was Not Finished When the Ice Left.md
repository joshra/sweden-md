# Land Uplift in Sweden and a Coastline Still in Motion: The Map Was Not Finished When the Ice Left

> **30-second overview:** The immense weight of the last ice sheet disappeared long ago, but the Swedish crust is still rebounding. Along the northern Gulf of Bothnia, land can rise by roughly a centimetre a year: islands join, bays become lakes, height references need dates, and climate-driven sea-level rise simultaneously redraws the same map.

## Why it matters

When readers in Taiwan think about a changing coast, typhoons, erosion, subsidence, and sea-level rise may come first. Sweden must also prepare for rising seas, but our coastline has another movement running in the opposite direction and on a much longer clock: the ground itself is rising.

Growing up in Sweden, I encountered `landhöjning` as a familiar piece of school geography. Only after moving to Taiwan did I fully appreciate how unusual it is. Apparently stable Swedish bedrock still carries a memory of the ice sheet's weight. That process shapes the High Coast World Heritage landscape, but it also enters harbours, water-level observations, surveying, coastal planning, and climate-risk assessment. To understand land uplift is to understand that Sweden's national basemap is not yet finished.

## The ice left; the crust is still rebounding

During the last Ice Age, a continental ice sheet several kilometres thick weighed down Scandinavia and depressed the crust. The ice began melting around 20,000 years ago and had left Scandinavia roughly 10,000 years ago. The response of material within the Earth is slow, however, and the crust continues to move towards a new equilibrium. The process is commonly called post-glacial rebound or glacial isostatic adjustment.

It does not proceed at one national rate. Lantmäteriet reports the fastest uplift along the northern Gulf of Bothnia, at roughly 10 to 11 millimetres per year, compared with about one millimetre per year in the far south of Skåne. A few millimetres sound negligible until they are extended across a lifetime, the service life of a harbour, or several centuries of settlement.

In the eighteenth century, Anders Celsius proposed cutting water-level marks into coastal rock. Observers could not yet determine whether the water was diminishing or the land was rising. Today, long water-level records are combined with precision levelling, permanent GNSS stations, and evidence from former shorelines. From rock marks to satellites, the way Sweden has learned to measure its moving ground is also a history of science.

## Bays become lakes and islands grow together

The clearest landscape expression lies around the High Coast and the Kvarken Archipelago on opposite sides of the Gulf of Bothnia. UNESCO lists Sweden's Höga Kusten and Finland's Kvarken Archipelago as one World Heritage property because they preserve and continue to display the combined effects of glaciation and rapid crustal rebound. Since the ice retreated, the High Coast has risen by about 285 metres relative to the sea, making it a key landscape for understanding the process.

Land uplift is not confined to ancient shorelines high on a slope. As shallow seabeds emerge, new islets appear, separate islands may join, peninsulas extend, and bays can be cut off into lakes before developing into wetlands and peatlands. A narrow channel navigable in one generation may need dredging in another, or eventually become dry land.

This movement also changes how history is read. An old settlement, burial ground, or harbour site now inland may have stood beside the water when it was used. Swedish archaeology and landscape history cannot simply project today's shoreline backwards; they must reconstruct the relative position of land and sea for the period in question.

## Even a height needs a date

Continuing ground movement means that “height above sea level” is not an eternally fixed number. Lantmäteriet's national height system depends on stable benchmarks, a gravity-based reference surface, and a defined reference epoch. Because northern and southern Sweden rise at different rates, the height relationship between two places slowly changes. Surveying, maps, engineering, and precision positioning must account for this when data from different epochs are combined.

The official NKG2016LU uplift model integrates repeated precision levelling with permanent GNSS observations and is used across the Nordic and Baltic countries. It sounds specialised, yet it reveals a characteristic form of Swedish governance: a natural process is observed over the long term and converted into a shared reference system so that roads, buildings, coastal works, and public data can agree.

Nor does newly exposed ground mean that property rights extend in whichever way seems most intuitive. Swedish boundaries in coastal waters can still depend on historical surveys, jointly owned waters, and legal definitions. A moving natural boundary ultimately needs records and institutions to explain who may use, manage, or alter the meeting place between land and water.

## Rising land does not cancel rising seas

The most important mistake to avoid is treating land uplift as proof that Sweden need not worry about climate change. Crustal rebound and climate-driven sea-level rise occur simultaneously, and the combined result differs along the Swedish coast.

SMHI distinguishes absolute uplift relative to the Earth's centre, uplift relative to the geoid, and apparent uplift relative to actual mean sea level. Along some rapidly rising central and northern coasts, land uplift currently offsets or exceeds the local effect of sea-level rise. In southern Sweden, uplift is slower and rising seas already contribute more directly to flooding, erosion, groundwater change, and saltwater intrusion. If global sea-level rise accelerates, the balance may eventually shift even where uplift offers a temporary local counterweight today.

There is therefore no single Swedish answer to coastal change. Local planning has to ask how quickly the ground is rising at a particular site, how high the sea may rise under different emissions pathways, and how extreme water levels can compound the trend. Land uplift is not a rebuttal to climate risk; it is an essential local variable in calculating that risk.

## Contemporary significance

`Landhöjning` brings very slow geological time into present-day administration. It shows that a coastline is not a fixed border, a height is not a number without an epoch, and a World Heritage landscape is not frozen scenery. Sweden's maps, harbours, and coastal communities are still moving within forces left by the last Ice Age.

For readers in Taiwan, the revealing point is less how much “new land” Sweden gains than how public governance acknowledges a changing basemap. When crustal rebound, rising seas, ecological succession, and human use occur together, sound planning cannot select the most convenient trend. It must keep measuring—and accept that different places will produce different answers.
