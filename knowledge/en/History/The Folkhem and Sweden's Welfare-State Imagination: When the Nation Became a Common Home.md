# The Folkhem and Sweden's Welfare-State Imagination: When the Nation Became a Common Home

> **30-second overview:** `Folkhemmet`, literally "the people's home," became widely known through Per Albin Hansson's 1928 parliamentary speech. It imagined Swedish society as a household with neither favourites nor stepchildren. Housing, healthcare, education, and social-security reforms later carried that metaphor into everyday life, but eugenic policy, institutional segregation, and normalising governance reveal that the home's door was never equally open to everyone.

## Why it matters

Sweden's welfare state is often introduced in Taiwan as a list of benefits: what support exists, how much leave people receive, or how healthcare is financed. `Folkhemmet` invites an earlier question. Why should a society understand the housing, health, and security of strangers as a shared responsibility? The answer lies not only in institutions but in political language. Once the nation is described as a home, equality becomes more than distribution. It becomes a moral claim that members should not be treated as favourites and neglected children.

As a Swede living in Taiwan, I also want to make the other side explicit. A home is a warm metaphor, but it draws boundaries. Who was recognised as a proper, healthy, productive member? Who was treated as someone to correct, segregate, or manage? Understanding `Folkhemmet` means neither celebrating nor dismissing the welfare state. It means seeing how universal protection and powerful standards of normal life could grow together.

## 1928: how a home became political language

Per Albin Hansson did not invent the word `Folkhemmet`, but his use of it in a 1928 parliamentary debate made it one of twentieth-century Sweden's most durable political metaphors. His ideal home had no privileged favourites or unwanted stepchildren; its members met one another through equality, consideration, cooperation, and mutual help. The speech matters not because it instantly created a welfare state, but because it translated a critique of class society into an everyday relationship almost everyone could recognise.

It also signalled a social-democratic shift. The project was not simply to construct a separate workers' world outside existing society, but to remake the nation as a more equal civic community. The home connected class reform with national belonging. Yet a speech offered direction, not proof of completion. Each reform still depended on elections, organisation, taxation, municipalities, and administrative capacity.

## From metaphor to rooms, bathrooms, and social security

Nordiska museet identifies the 1930s through the 1960s as the characteristic `Folkhemmet` period. Overcrowding, poverty, and long working days in the early industrial society were increasingly challenged by a public vision of good housing, healthcare, holidays, and leisure. Postwar industry created favourable fiscal and productive conditions, while state loans, housing standards, and large-scale construction translated abstract equality into kitchens, bathrooms, hot water, storage, and communal laundries.

That material layer is important for Taiwan-facing readers. A welfare state does not exist only in transfers recorded by a social-insurance agency. It is also present in doorway widths, daylight, hygiene, household movement, and whether a day contains time to rest. The strength of `Folkhemmet` was its ability to turn the physical conditions of private life into matters that public policy could measure, design, and improve. Full social membership acquired a concrete space.

## Expanding equality, enforcing normality

Once the state began defining a good home, a healthy family, and a proper citizen, care could sit uncomfortably close to control. Population policy, eugenic thought, sterilisation laws, and institutional care exposed people classified as outside the norm to serious violations. Forum för levande historia cautions against reducing compulsory sterilisation to one party's simple "dark side of the welfare state": the laws had broad political support, and similar policies existed elsewhere. That wider responsibility does not diminish the state violence experienced by those affected.

The lives of disabled people reveal another gap in the common-home promise. Nordiska museet records that many still lived in poor institutions in the mid-twentieth century, that modern housing was commonly inaccessible, and that people receiving poor relief did not gain voting rights until 1948. The "everyone" of `Folkhemmet` was therefore not a completed category. It remained a promise repeatedly challenged by rights movements, democratic reform, and later generations.

## A historical word that is still contested

Sweden today is not the society of the 1950s. Migration, European integration, market reforms, ageing, and housing segregation have changed the conditions of shared responsibility. Political actors still reuse `Folkhemmet`: some invoke universal welfare and social solidarity, while others narrow the home toward cultural belonging or nostalgia for a past that was never equally safe for everyone.

I would not offer `Folkhemmet` to Taiwanese readers as a Nordic formula ready for import. It is more useful as a historical question. Can a society imagine itself as a common home without allowing one family form, body, or identity to decide who qualifies as a full member? The most revealing part of Sweden's welfare-state history is not only how many institutions it built. It is that the promise of a place for everyone still has to be inspected and widened.
