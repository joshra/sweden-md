# The Great Swedish Emigration: When More Than a Million People Left Home Across the Atlantic

> **30-second overview:** From the mid-nineteenth century to around 1930, more than a million Swedes left home, most of them for North America. This was not simply a story of poor people chasing an American dream. Population pressure, land, religious freedom, transport, and letters from family worked together to reorganise Swedish society—and to show that the Sweden now associated with stability was once a country many people could not imagine a future in.

## Why it matters

Swedish history is often narrated abroad as a quick leap from industrialisation to democracy and the welfare state. That smooth arc leaves out the fact that nineteenth-century Sweden had not yet created secure work, housing, or social protection for everyone. For many families, the most practical way to change life was not to wait for the country to improve, but to leave it.

As a Swede living in Taiwan, I think the label “migration country” should not apply only to the United States as a destination. Sweden was also made by departure: emptied rural communities, families divided by an ocean, letters and money returning home, and the political question of why so many people chose to go. That history gives present-day Swedish debates about migration and integration an uncomfortable but necessary mirror.

## More than a million departures: understanding the numbers

Two totals commonly appear in official Swedish material. The National Archives describes about 1.3 million Swedes emigrating from the 1830s into the 1930s, primarily to North America, using registered archival sources. Sweden's official national portal uses an estimate of about 1.5 million for the broader Great Emigration between 1850 and 1930. The figures need not directly contradict each other: periods, destinations, and definitions differ.

Whichever measure we use, the scale transformed society. Sweden had roughly 3.5 million inhabitants in 1850. In the peak year of 1887 alone, more than 50,000 people left. The moving certificates, passenger lists, agent records, and population accounts held by the National Archives also take us beyond the headline number. They let us trace migration to a parish, a ship, and sometimes an individual name.

## Why people left: poverty, freedom, and the future

Poverty and scarce land were central pressures. Sweden's nineteenth-century population grew, while an agrarian society could not provide every child with enough land to sustain a household. Bad harvests, low wages, and insecure work made families more vulnerable. Against that reality, North American land and wages held obvious appeal.

Yet “they were poor” flattens human choice. Religious minorities sought greater freedom. Some people resisted political and social hierarchy; others were drawn by adventure, gold, or the possibility of beginning again. Emigration was both escape and a vote about the future: a decision about how to weigh a familiar home against unknown risks and the life a next generation might gain.

That complexity may feel familiar to Taiwan-facing readers. Movement across an ocean rarely has one cause. Family responsibility, work, liberty, and imagination can all be folded into the same ticket.

## From parish to port: migration as a network

Leaving did not mean stepping directly from the Swedish countryside into the American Midwest. Legal emigrants commonly obtained moving documents from their parish before entering a chain of agents, railways, ports, and ocean routes. Gothenburg was a major point of departure. Earlier travellers often crossed the North Sea to Britain before transferring to an Atlantic service. Only after the Swedish American Line was founded in 1915 did a regular Swedish passenger route connect Gothenburg and New York directly.

More importantly, people did not move as isolated individuals. Those already abroad sent “America letters” describing work, land, and daily life, sometimes enclosing money for passage. Trusted information reduced uncertainty, turning one person's departure into a route followed by siblings, neighbours, and others from the same parish. Many Swedes regrouped in places such as Minnesota, where language, churches, newspapers, and food helped build new local communities.

The Swedish Emigrant Institute now preserves letters, diaries, photographs, biographies, and oral histories. These collections show why migration history cannot be reduced to exit statistics. It also belongs to those waiting for a reply, those who stayed, and families changing on both sides of the Atlantic.

## What remained: modern Sweden was shaped by absences

Mass emigration ebbed during the 1920s as the United States tightened immigration restrictions and Swedish industrialisation, wages, and living conditions improved. Not everyone left permanently. Some returned, while other families remained distributed across the Atlantic. The story is therefore not simply “Sweden lost people and America gained them,” but a continuing circulation of knowledge, money, and culture.

Its deepest political legacy was to make departure a question directed at Swedish society. When people expressed dissatisfaction with their feet, landholding, class hierarchy, religious liberty, working conditions, and political participation became harder to treat as natural and fixed. Later democracy and welfare reform cannot be attributed to emigration alone, but neither can the histories be fully separated. The exodus exposed how the old Sweden failed to make room for everyone.

Looking back, I would not describe this as a dark prologue before the welfare state. It is more like a seam that remains visible: modern Sweden was built both by people who stayed to reform it and by those who left, wrote home, and sometimes returned.
