# The Million Programme and Modern Housing in Sweden: Building One Million Homes in a Decade

> **30-second overview:** Between 1965 and 1974, Sweden completed 1,005,578 homes—nearly one fifth of the country's current housing stock. The `Miljonprogrammet`, or Million Programme, is often reduced to monotonous tower blocks. Its deeper story is how a welfare state made housing shortage measurable, financeable, and buildable at scale, then passed the costs of speed and standardisation to later generations.

## Why it matters

As a Swede living in Taiwan, I often find two images of Swedish housing difficult to connect. One is the bright Nordic home with a proper kitchen and generous green space. The other is the concrete suburb described in news reports as a "problem area." The Million Programme joins these pictures. Its original problem was not architectural style but the genuine housing shortage produced by postwar urbanisation, population growth, and industrial demand for labour.

It also shows that a right to decent housing does not descend from a political slogan. Parliamentary credit and subsidies, municipal land and planning, industrial building methods, transport, schools, and shops all had to work together. Yet when a country tries to close decades of shortage in ten years, scale magnifies mistakes too. This history deserves neither dismissal as a failed concrete utopia nor celebration through the number one million alone.

## From housing shortage to a measurable national target

The Million Programme conventionally names the building period from 1965 through 1974. Boverket records 1,005,578 completed homes of all types during those ten years. It was not a wholly separate programme invented overnight. Sweden had raised housing output steadily since the 1950s; the million marked a clear production target that accelerated existing policy. The state shaped construction through housing finance and budget allocations, while municipalities had to prepare land and long-term plans.

The political promise behind the number mattered more. Housing policy in the 1960s held that the whole population should have healthy, spacious, well-planned, suitably equipped homes of good quality at reasonable cost. Private difficulty in "finding somewhere to live" became a public question of standards. How many people shared a room? Did a home have a bathroom, hot water, and a usable kitchen? Could a family afford it? The welfare state entered walls, pipes, and room dimensions.

## Not only towers: many homes, one production logic

For many Swedes, `Miljonprogrammet` still evokes rows of high-rise blocks at the urban edge. Boverket notes, however, that three-storey slab blocks were the most common building type and roughly one third of all new homes were detached houses or other small houses. A "Million Programme area" is therefore not one architectural form but an entire decade's housing stock.

The shared feature was a production logic: large sites, long series, less labour, and lower cost. From 1966, advance approval for housing loans particularly encouraged uniform projects of at least one thousand homes. Prefabricated components, repeated plans, and mechanised construction made the output possible. They could also produce monotony, excessive scale, or housing completed before local services matured. Standardisation was both an instrument of social reform and a source of later criticism.

## A new home is not yet a complete city

The new housing did allow many families to leave overcrowded and poorly equipped homes. SCB data also show that larger dwellings became more common during the Million Programme period. For family life, that could mean a room for the children and a kitchen, bathroom, and storage no longer treated as luxuries. Looking only at ageing façades today can hide how modern these homes felt on arrival.

Yet a well-equipped flat does not automatically make a connected city. Some large estates were distant from jobs, cultural life, and urban centres, with concentrated tenure and housing types. As employment, migration, and housing markets changed, spatial separation could overlap with differences in income and background. Boverket's study of several large estates discusses exclusion, poor reputation, and low attractiveness, while arguing that renewal should begin with residents' needs and local identity. Architecture alone cannot carry the blame, and residents must not be treated as the people who need "fixing."

## Fifty years later: renovating a social contract

Much of this housing is now more than half a century old. Pipes, façades, ventilation, and energy performance need work, but technical renovation quickly becomes a distributional question. Who pays? Does energy improvement raise rents? Can residents shape a project before work begins, rather than receive notice of a more expensive home after completion? When buildings of one era age together, concentrated construction capacity becomes concentrated maintenance pressure.

The lesson for Taiwanese readers is not a simple verdict on whether government should build many homes at once. A better question is whether speed can leave room for mixed housing, long-term maintenance resources, complete public services, and residents' power to change their own neighbourhood. Sweden's million homes prove that public institutions can transform living conditions. Their second half-century proves that completion of construction is not completion of the task.
