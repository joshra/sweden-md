# Kulning and Sweden's Women's Herding Calls: When Singing Was a Working Technology

> **30-second overview:** `Kulning` is a high-register calling technique developed around Sweden's seasonal herding farms. Herders used distinct calls to gather cattle and goats, signal their location, ask for help, or communicate across long distances. It is often presented today as an enchanting “Nordic sound,” but its more revealing history lies before the stage: young women shaped it as practical knowledge among labour, animals, and outdoor acoustics.

## Why It Matters

When I explain `kulning` to friends in Taiwan, the most useful comparison is not classical singing. It is the calls, whistles, and signals that people develop while working in mountains or across fields. A voice had to travel through forest and over slopes so that a wandering cow could hear it—or so that someone at another summer farm would know where the caller was. Its height and penetrating clarity answered distance before they answered an audience.

That perspective corrects two easy misunderstandings. Swedish folk music did not all begin as entertainment; some sounds were tools. And cultural heritage consists not only of famous composers or surviving instruments, but also of women's techniques that were long treated as ordinary labour and therefore rarely attached to celebrated names. To hear `kulning` historically is to hear who helped sustain food production in Sweden's forest and mountain regions.

## The Summer Farm: A Sound Grown from an Agricultural System

`Kulning` cannot be separated from the `fäbod`, a seasonal herding system. Livestock were moved in summer to forest or mountain pasture away from the village or home farm, leaving nearby land available for crops and winter fodder. At the summer farm, people milked animals, made butter and cheese, reduced whey, and found livestock moving through an open landscape.

Young women performed much of this work. Materials from Sweden's Institute for Language and Folklore (Isof) explain that different calls could lead animals, bring them home, warn them, calm them, or maintain a familiar kind of “conversation.” `Kulning` was therefore not one fixed song. It was a working vocabulary adjusted to animals, location, and circumstance—sometimes tones alone, sometimes names or short phrases, and often improvised.

Calling it a “cow song” is convenient but suggests a complete composition. A melodic herding call is more accurate. The word is related to the dialect verb `kula`, to call cattle with a singing voice; regional terms also include `kauka` and `köla`. Those variations matter. They remind us that the practice was not one nationally standardized style carried unchanged through time, but a family of local practices later gathered under a shared name.

## High, Straight, and Far-Carrying: A Landscape Shaped the Technique

`Kulning` is often recognized by its high register, forceful and precise onset, and straight tone with little or no vibrato. Singers use the transition between chest and head resonance to project over several kilometres. There was no amplification: forest, valley, lake surface, weather, and distance all participated in the performance. A good sound was not beautiful in the abstract; it worked in a place.

Women at the summer farms also called to one another. Agreed signals could say that a lost animal had been found, help was needed, or the caller was in a particular location. Birch-bark horns and cattle or goat horns could warn that wolves or bears were nearby or mark the beginning and end of work. Voice and instrument formed a low-technology but finely differentiated communications network.

For modern listeners accustomed to dividing music into “works” and “performances,” this sound is difficult to classify. It is music and information at once. It carries an individual's timbre while needing to be understood correctly by animals and other people. That tells a richer story than the claim that Swedes simply sing well: aesthetic form can emerge from bodies repeatedly solving practical problems.

## From Worksite to Folk-Music Stage

As the economic importance of the traditional `fäbod` system declined during the twentieth century, its work music lost the setting in which it had been transmitted. Younger people once learned by imitating older workers while caring for livestock. When the work and living environment disappeared, that ordinary chain of learning broke. Isof's recordings and interviews are valuable for precisely this reason: the archive preserves not only melodies but evidence of where, for whom, and why a sound was used.

Today, `kulning` continues through active summer farms, folk-music stages, workshops, and music education. Moving onto a stage is neither simple decline nor a revival without loss. Performance gives more people a chance to hear and learn the technique, but it can also detach the spectacular high sound from cattle, dairy work, forest grazing, and women's long working days.

Contemporary transmission should therefore preserve more than vocal production. Learning to make an impressive sound and understanding how that sound once organized work are two different layers of cultural knowledge.

## After National Listing: Preserve the Relationships as Well as the Sound

In 2025, `kulning` entered Sweden's national inventory of intangible cultural heritage. That did not turn it into a completed national treasure that should no longer change. The inventory identifies a living practice still performed, taught, and adapted, while making its vulnerable conditions more visible.

Recordings alone are incomplete preservation if there are no open landscapes for the sound to answer, no active farms, no relationships with animals, and no communities of learning. At the same time, today's singers do not need to pretend they live in the nineteenth century. `Kulning` can enter new compositions and stages, provided innovation is not presented as ahistorical “Nordic magic.”

For Taiwanese readers, that leads to a familiar question: how many oral sounds from agricultural and local labour have gone unrecognized as knowledge because they were too ordinary, too closely associated with women, or too difficult to put into notation? The value of `kulning` is not only how far the voice carries. It lies in our willingness to follow the sound back toward people whose work was hidden in the landscape.
