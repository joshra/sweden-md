# Reindeer Husbandry and Sami Land Rights: Northern Sweden Is Not Empty Wilderness

> **30-second overview:** In Sweden, reindeer husbandry is not a winter postcard or ethnic decoration. It is a living system of Sami land use, cultural continuity, and legal rights. To understand it is to see northern Sweden as a landscape shaped by seasonal movement, mining, forestry, wind power, climate change, and Indigenous rights.

## Why it matters

When I explain northern Sweden from Taiwan, the first misunderstanding I often want to remove is the idea that the north is “empty.” Even within Sweden, the north is often described through state and industry language: forests, minerals, hydropower, wind resources. But for Sami reindeer husbandry, this land is not blank and not simply waiting to be planned. It is made of seasonal routes, grazing knowledge, family memory, and legal disputes.

This topic matters not because reindeer feel “Nordic,” but because reindeer husbandry exposes several central Swedish tensions at once. How does a country that speaks strongly about nature and human rights handle Indigenous rights? How does a country pursuing the green transition avoid placing its costs on northern land and Sami communities? And can a highly capable administrative state really hear land knowledge that urban majorities may not easily understand?

## Reindeer husbandry is not just northern livestock farming

Taiwanese readers can easily misread Swedish reindeer husbandry if they imagine ordinary livestock farming. The system is not built around keeping animals inside one fixed pasture. It depends on seasonal movement between grazing lands. Reindeer need winter grounds, summer grounds, migration routes, resting areas, and a landscape that remains passable. The scarce resource is therefore not only land area; it is ecological and spatial continuity.

The Swedish reindeer-herding area covers close to half of the country, but that does not mean half of Sweden is a freely usable pasture. Sametinget explains that not all land inside the `renskötselområde` is suitable for grazing, and that rights differ between year-round lands, winter grazing lands, and concession areas. Sweden has 51 `samebyar`. The word is often translated as “Sami villages,” but a `sameby` is not an ordinary village. It is a legal, administrative, and economic association that organizes reindeer husbandry within a defined geographic area.

Another overlooked point is that Sweden has no wild reindeer. According to Sametinget, every reindeer in Sweden is owned by someone, while unmarked reindeer belong collectively to the local `sameby`. The ear mark, or `renmärke`, is not just a technical mark. It belongs to a system of ownership, family continuity, and collective management.

## Legal rights: from immemorial use to modern state rules

The Swedish Reindeer Husbandry Act states that persons of Sami descent may use land and water, under the law, to sustain themselves and their reindeer. The right belongs to the Sami people and is based on `urminnes hävd`, a Swedish legal concept rooted in long-standing historical use. At the same time, the Act says that the right is exercised by members of a `sameby`, tying cultural rights to membership, livelihood, collective organization, and state law.

That tension is important for Taiwan readers. The Swedish state recognizes reindeer-herding rights, but the form of recognition also defines who may exercise the right, how, and within which administrative framework. Indigenous rights are not solved by saying “respect tradition.” They enter concrete legal boundaries, membership rules, land conflicts, and public procedures.

The 2020 Girjas judgment is one of the most important recent examples. The Swedish Supreme Court held that Girjas sameby, on the basis of `urminnes hävd`, has the right within its specific area to grant small-game hunting and fishing rights, and that the state may not grant those rights there. The judgment did not solve every Sami land issue in Sweden, but it made one point hard to ignore: historical use is not cultural decoration; it can become a substantive right recognized by a court.

## Competing land use: the green transition also creates pressure

The pressures facing reindeer husbandry today do not come only from old-style development. Forestry, mining, hydropower, roads, railways, tourism, wind power, and large-carnivore management can all reshape the space available to reindeer. SLU’s reindeer-husbandry research focuses on climate change, other land uses, predators, and external stressors. These are not abstract issues; they affect winter grazing, lichen resources, migration routes, and everyday decisions made by herding families.

The hardest part is that many pressures arrive in the language of “public interest” or “sustainability.” Wind power and minerals may be framed as part of a low-carbon economy. Forests may be framed as bioresources. Railways and energy infrastructure may be framed as national competitiveness. But for reindeer husbandry, projects that cut migration routes, reduce winter pasture, or increase disturbance do not merely cause local inconvenience. They make the whole pastoral system harder to maintain.

This is one of the sharp lessons northern Sweden offers Taiwan: sustainability policy cannot look only at carbon. It must also look at land justice. If a transition gives urban consumers cleaner energy while Indigenous communities carry the cost of fragmented landscapes, it is not yet a complete sustainability transition.

## Reindeer husbandry plans: translating landscape knowledge into negotiation

One important Swedish tool is the `Renbruksplan`, or reindeer husbandry plan. Sametinget describes its purpose as giving `samebyar` a clear way to describe their land use for spatial planning, environmental impact assessment, consultation, negotiation, and dialogue. These plans combine remote sensing, grazing-land descriptions, external land-use factors, GPS data from reindeer, field inventories, and the RenGIS system.

The tool matters because it does not turn Sami knowledge into folklore. It helps bring knowledge that has often been carried through oral practice, experience, and seasonal memory into administrative, corporate, and legal discussions. For Taiwan readers, this is a problem of institutional translation. Participation is not only about being invited into the room. It is also about whether the people invited can use their own forms of knowledge to affect the decision.

But tools do not automatically equal power. Sweden adopted a consultation law on matters affecting the Sami people in 2022. Government, state agencies, and from 2024 local and regional authorities must consult on matters of special significance to the Sami people. This is institutional progress, but consultation is not a veto. The real question remains: when consultation meets mining, energy, and national-interest claims, where exactly is Sami knowledge placed in the decision-making hierarchy?

## What Taiwan readers can take from this

If reindeer husbandry is written only as “traditional culture,” the story becomes too soft. If it is written only as “development conflict,” it becomes too narrow. What it really reveals is a deeper problem in Swedish nature governance: nature is not a neutral background. It is made through memory, rights, labor, and institutions.

Taiwan also knows the tensions between mountain landscapes, Indigenous lands, energy transition, and state planning. Sweden does not provide an easy answer, but it offers a clear question: when a society says it respects nature and Indigenous peoples, can that respect enter courts, administrative procedures, environmental-impact data, mapping systems, and budgets? If not, it risks remaining only attractive value language.
