# Wetlands and Peatland Restoration in Sweden: Keeping Water, Carbon, and Biodiversity in the Landscape

> **30-second overview:** Swedish `våtmarker` (wetlands) include mires, peatlands, wet meadows, shore zones, and forested wet ground. They are less postcard-friendly than mountains or archipelagos, but they are central to understanding how Sweden governs water, carbon, biodiversity, and land use.

## Why it matters

When I explain Swedish nature from Taiwan, people usually ask first about forests, lakes, snow, or the northern lights. Wetlands rarely appear in that first image. But growing up with Swedish landscapes teaches a different lesson: much of nature is not neatly divided into forest or lake. It is a slow meeting of water, moss, peat, dwarf trees, and open ground.

For Taiwanese readers, wetlands matter because they shift the question from scenery to function. A place that once looked like difficult land to use may be storing water, holding carbon, filtering nutrients, supporting species, and preserving the consequences of earlier drainage for forestry and agriculture.

## What they are: Swedish landscapes between water and land

Swedish wetlands are not one landscape type. They may be wide northern mires, waterlogged forest depressions, wet meadows along rivers and lakes, or shallow habitats near coasts and islands. What they share is that water shapes the soil, plants, and microbial conditions over time, making them neither ordinary dry land nor open water.

That in-between quality is their value. Peatlands accumulate organic matter under oxygen-poor conditions, slowing the return of carbon to the atmosphere. Wetlands can also retain water, hold nutrients, and provide habitat for birds, insects, amphibians, and specialized plants. They are often quiet landscapes, but they concentrate several ecological functions in one place.

## The drainage history: when wetlands were land to be dried

Sweden's current restoration agenda cannot be understood without its long history of drainage. For agriculture, forestry, and land improvement, many wetlands were ditched and dried, weakening systems that had stored water, accumulated peat, and supported wetland species. This is not a uniquely Swedish story, but it carries special weight in a forest country where production and conservation often meet in the same landscape.

Drainage changes more than surface water. When peatlands dry, organic material can decompose and release greenhouse gases. Habitats shrink or fragment, species dependent on wet conditions lose ground, and downstream water quality and flood-regulation capacity may weaken. Sweden's growing attention to `återvätning` (rewetting) reflects the fact that wetland restoration sits at the intersection of climate, ecology, and water governance.

## How restoration works: putting water levels back into governance

Wetland restoration sounds like nature engineering, but it is also governance. A common approach is to block or adjust old drainage ditches, raise water levels, and restore water pathways so peat and wetland vegetation can return to more stable wet conditions. These measures require judgment about topography, water rights, forestry or agricultural interests, neighbouring land, and long-term responsibility.

That is why Swedish wetland policy is not simply a call for more nature protection. It involves national environmental objectives, county administrative boards, municipalities, landowners, forestry and agricultural actors. The important point for Taiwan is that restoration is not hard because people fail to understand that water matters. It is hard because changing water levels must be made compatible with law, finance, property, and local trust.

## Contemporary relevance: climate adaptation and biodiversity meet

Under climate change, wetlands have become more visible in Sweden's public reasoning. They can help landscapes retain water and moderate drought and flood risks. Rewetting drained peatlands is also treated as one pathway for reducing emissions, while wetlands remain habitat networks for many species. Wetlands therefore belong not only to conservation policy but also to climate policy, land policy, and local development.

Still, they should not be romanticized as a simple nature-based fix. Restoration can raise conflicts over land use, local economies, mosquitoes, maintenance responsibilities, and monitoring of actual effects. Sweden's more useful lesson is this: if a country's nature story focuses only on the productivity of dry land, it misses the wet, slow, commercially awkward places that quietly carry long-term public functions.
