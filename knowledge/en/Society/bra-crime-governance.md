# Brå and Sweden's Crime-Governance Knowledge Infrastructure: Why Public Safety Depends on Statistics, Surveys, and Local Prevention

> **30-second overview:** When Taiwanese readers think about public safety, the first questions are often whether there are enough police or whether punishments are harsh enough. Brå represents another layer of the story in Sweden: the state also needs to know how crime is counted, how insecurity is experienced, and how prevention is organised locally, or else crime politics collapses into slogans and mood swings.

## Why It Matters

Living in Taiwan, I often see crime debates pulled quickly toward two poles. One is whether the numbers can be trusted. The other is whether the state should simply respond more forcefully. Sweden has those arguments too, especially after years of pressure around gang violence, shootings, and bombings. But if we stop there, we miss a more basic question: what kind of institutional knowledge allows a country to understand crime in the first place and turn that understanding into public action?

Brå matters because it sits at the centre of that question. It is a knowledge agency inside the justice system. It produces official crime statistics, runs victim and self-report surveys, evaluates reforms, and supports local crime-prevention work. In that sense, Brå is not just publishing background research from the sidelines. It is one of the instruments through which the Swedish state calibrates how it talks about crime and what it tries to do about it.

## What Brå Is: A Knowledge Centre for the Justice System, Not Another Police Force

Brå, the Swedish National Council for Crime Prevention, is a government agency under the Ministry of Justice. Its official English presentation describes it as a centre of knowledge for the criminal justice system, with a mandate to develop knowledge for criminal policy and the justice sector while also promoting crime prevention and efforts against violent extremism.

That position is important. Brå does not investigate crimes and it is not a court. Its role is to turn fragmented information about crime into a basis for public judgement. The government needs to know which trends are changing and which reforms work. Municipalities need to understand the structure of the problems they face. The wider public needs a source that is less vulnerable to selective storytelling. By institutionalising that function, Sweden is effectively saying that crime governance is not only about arresting offenders. It is also about maintaining a durable capacity to produce, test, and revise knowledge.

## Why Brå Owns Official Crime Statistics: Because Safety Numbers Are Never Neutral

One of Brå’s central responsibilities is Sweden’s official criminal-justice statistics. Its statistics page states that the agency must ensure that these statistics are objective, documented, and accompanied by quality declarations, while adapting them to the needs of different users. That may sound technical, but it is also deeply political, because crime numbers are exceptionally easy to misuse.

Brå has published a dedicated English explainer on why Swedish crime statistics should not be read through simplistic international comparison. In Sweden, all reported events are initially recorded as crimes even if some later turn out not to constitute offences. Repeated offences against the same victim are generally counted separately. Attempted offences are in most cases counted together with completed offences. None of this automatically means that Sweden is more dangerous than somewhere else. It means the recording logic is different. For Taiwanese readers, that is a useful reminder that whenever a headline says a country’s crime is "surging," the first question should be how the number was constructed.

## From Administrative Records to Victim Surveys: Crime Governance Also Needs Social Perception

If official statistics answer what the system records, the Swedish Crime Survey answers what people actually experience. Brå lists these large-scale surveys as part of its official statistical responsibility. Their purpose is not only to measure self-reported victimisation, but also to track worry about crime, confidence in the justice system, and victims’ experience of contact with authorities.

This is a very Swedish way of governing: not just counting cases that entered the system, but also asking how society experiences insecurity. Reporting rates, policing priorities, and legal definitions all shape administrative data. If a state relies only on those records, it risks mistaking recorded crime for the total reality of crime. By combining official statistics with victim surveys, Brå helps build a more layered national sense of what is happening.

## The Post-2023 Shift: Municipal Crime Prevention Became a Legal Duty

Brå has become even more important in recent years because the institutional framework changed. On 1 July 2023, Sweden brought into force the Act on Municipal Responsibility for Crime Prevention Work, `Lag (2023:196) om kommuners ansvar för brottsförebyggande arbete`. The law requires municipalities to produce local crime assessments, determine the need for measures, adopt action plans, and maintain coordination functions.

Brå’s crime-prevention pages and its 2026 status report both show how this reform has made prevention work more formalised at local, regional, and national level. Brå and the county administrative boards now play a major support and coordination role in helping municipalities carry out knowledge-based prevention. This is a familiar Swedish governance instinct: when a social problem becomes politically urgent, the answer is not only louder rhetoric. It is also to require local government to describe the problem clearly, organise collaboration, and turn prevention into an administrative routine that can be followed up.

## A Taiwan-Facing Takeaway: Crime Politics Without a Knowledge Base Becomes Volatile

What makes Brå worth understanding for Taiwanese readers is that it widens the frame of what public safety actually is. Safety is not only about police, courts, or prisons. It is also about how statistics are made, whether people are willing to report crime, whether municipalities can coordinate across sectors, and whether policies are evaluated instead of merely announced.

Sweden has by no means solved all of these problems. The country’s recent struggles with serious violence and organised criminal networks are real. But Brå adds an institutional stabiliser to the system. It cannot prevent political conflict, but it makes it more possible for that conflict to return to data, methods, and concrete measures. To me, that is one of the most recognisable features of Swedish institutional culture: public problems are not supposed to be managed by posture alone. They are supposed to rest on knowledge that can be examined, revised, and accumulated.
