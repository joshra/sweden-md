# The Right-of-Centre Bloc After the Tido Agreement: Government, Support, and Policy Gravity

> **30-second overview:** Sweden's current right-of-centre government is formed by the Moderates, Christian Democrats, and Liberals, but depends on Sweden Democrat support. The Tido Agreement matters because policy influence extends beyond formal cabinet seats.

## Why it matters

If you only read the cabinet list, it is easy to underestimate the Sweden Democrats' role. In post-2022 Swedish politics, being outside cabinet does not mean being outside power. The Tido Agreement wrote support into a policy arrangement, so the government must be read through both cabinet composition and parliamentary support.

That is central for 2026. Voters are not only judging Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson's government. They are also judging whether this model of three cabinet parties plus one large support party should continue.

## Three cabinet parties and one large external party

After the 2022 election, the Moderates, Christian Democrats, and Liberals formed a minority government. The Sweden Democrats did not enter cabinet, but they were the largest party in the right-of-centre parliamentary base and supported the government through the Tido Agreement.

This created an asymmetrical arrangement: the prime minister's party was not the largest right-bloc party, and the largest support party stayed outside cabinet. The benefit is role separation between formal office and policy leverage. The cost is blurred responsibility.

## Migration and public order as shared language

After the Tido Agreement, migration, crime, law enforcement, and welfare access became the strongest shared language of right-of-centre cooperation. The Sweden Democrats had long centred these issues, while the Moderates also made public safety and state capacity part of their governing credibility.

But the right bloc is not uniform. The Liberals must retain a liberal identity and education profile. The Christian Democrats bring family, care, and value politics. The Moderates must remain a prime-ministerial and fiscal-governance party. The Sweden Democrats must show they can shape state policy, not only protest against it.

## Internal tensions inside the right

The shared interests are clear: stay in power, limit immigration, strengthen public safety, and project administrative competence. The tensions are also clear. The EU, climate policy, media freedom, education, cultural politics, and market liberalism can separate liberal-conservative and national-conservative priorities.

The 2026 election therefore tests not only right versus left, but the durability of cooperation inside the right. If the Liberals or Christian Democrats approach the threshold, the bloc arithmetic becomes fragile. If the Sweden Democrats grow further, the bloc's policy gravity may move even more toward their agenda.

## The agreement model is on the ballot

The Tido Agreement introduced a model in which a non-cabinet party can have major policy influence. In 2026, the question is whether voters accept the results of that model, and whether the right-of-centre parties are willing to renew or redesign it.

For readers in Taiwan, this is a useful reminder: in parliamentary systems, power does not only sit in executive office. It also sits with the parties that can keep a government alive.
