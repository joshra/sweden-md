# Nuclear Power and Sweden's Long-Term Electricity Choice: Low-Carbon Stability, Democratic Reversal, and Waste Responsibility

> **30-second overview:** Sweden has six operating nuclear reactors, together supplying roughly 30 percent of its electricity. This stable, low-carbon source was once placed on a path toward phase-out; today the state is prepared to share the risks of new construction. Nuclear power is therefore not merely an engineering option, but a long test of democratic promises, public finance, safety regulation, and responsibility for spent fuel.

## Why it matters

Taiwanese readers looking at Swedish energy often notice hydropower, wind power, and climate policy first. Without nuclear power, however, it is difficult to understand how Sweden has maintained an electricity mix with relatively little fossil generation—or why electrifying industry, north-south transmission constraints, and new generation now produce such intense political debate.

As a Swede living in Taiwan, I recognize the same temptation in both countries' energy arguments: everyone would like one sentence to settle whether nuclear power is the answer or the problem. Swedish experience refuses that comfort. Nuclear energy provides large volumes of stable, low-carbon electricity, but it also extends accident risk, major investment commitments, regulatory capacity, and spent-fuel responsibility far beyond any single government's term.

## From twelve reactors to today's six

Between 1972 and 1985, Swedish power companies commissioned twelve large reactors. Nuclear power once generated around half of the country's electricity and, together with northern hydropower, became a low-carbon backbone for industry and households.

Six reactors remain in operation today, spread across the Forsmark, Oskarshamn, and Ringhals sites. They provide roughly 30 percent of Swedish electricity. The share changes with maintenance, demand, and annual production from other sources, so one year's figure matters less than the system function: large, predictable volumes of electricity that can run continuously for long periods.

Sweden's low-carbon electricity system was never delivered by one heroic technology. Hydropower supplies dispatchable flexibility, wind produces large volumes when conditions allow, and nuclear offers stable output; the grid must still handle regional transmission, reserves, maintenance, and demand peaks. Transition is a problem of composition, not a moral prize awarded to each source in isolation.

## The 1980 referendum did not end the argument

After the 1979 Three Mile Island accident in the United States, Sweden held a nuclear referendum in 1980. All three alternatives pointed toward phase-out, although at different speeds and under different conditions. Parliament then decided that nuclear expansion should not go beyond the twelve reactors already operating, completed, or under construction, and that phase-out should proceed while accounting for electricity supply, employment, and welfare. A deadline of 2010 for the final closure was also articulated.

Yet electricity demand, climate goals, technical lifetimes, and party positions kept changing. The last reactor did not close in 2010. Sweden later removed the ban on replacement reactors, while electricity policy shifted from a target framed around “renewable” power to one framed around “fossil-free” power, reopening political space for nuclear energy.

This history should not be reduced either to democratic betrayal or pragmatic triumph. It is better understood as a lesson in democratic governance. An advisory referendum can set the direction of an era, but it cannot freeze the electricity system four decades later. Conversely, the ability to change policy does not make earlier commitments disposable. Energy democracy is hardest where new judgments must still account for old choices.

## Stable electricity comes with responsibility on a different timescale

Responsibility does not end when a reactor stops generating. A basic principle of Sweden's Nuclear Activities Act is that those producing nuclear waste must also ensure its safe management and final disposal. Operators must also make funds available for decommissioning and the management of nuclear waste and spent fuel.

Around 8,000 tonnes of highly radioactive spent fuel are currently held at the central interim storage facility, Clab, in Oskarshamn. In 2022, the government approved a spent-fuel repository and encapsulation plant, and an environmental court issued a permit with conditions in 2024. That does not mean the facilities are complete. The Swedish Radiation Safety Authority must continue reviewing and approving successive stages from construction through trial operation to routine operation.

This chain captures something characteristic of Swedish public governance. Declaring a technical solution does not close the question; operator responsibility, dedicated finance, environmental permission, safety regulation, and years of review must remain connected and accountable. Nuclear waste outlasts companies, parties, and perhaps today's institutions. A “solution” can only mean preventing responsibility from disappearing between generations.

## New nuclear is back—but risk allocation arrives first

The Swedish government has recently built a policy framework for new nuclear power. Since August 2025, companies have been able to apply for state support through government loans and two-way contracts for difference. The support ceiling covers about 5,000 MW of capacity, described as equivalent to four large-scale reactors.

The careful wording matters. A financing ceiling is not four completed plants, nor a guaranteed construction schedule. It instead reveals that new nuclear projects are difficult to finance through ordinary markets alone. Construction takes a long time, capital costs are high, and future electricity prices are uncertain. If society wants the long-term system value, government must explain which risks belong to taxpayers, electricity users, and investors.

Sweden's policy reversal is therefore not a return to the 1970s. Today's questions include safe lifetime extension, new technologies, permitting speed, public financial exposure, coordination with wind and grid investment, and whether waste responsibility expands coherently with any new reactors. For Taiwan, the useful lesson is not to copy a Swedish energy ratio. It is to demand that every long-term energy choice state its costs, risks, and responsibilities in full.
