# Duodji and Sami Cultural Self-Determination: Keeping Objects, Knowledge, and Livelihood Together

> **30-second overview:** `Duodji` is often translated as Sami handicraft, but it encompasses more than a beautiful object. It carries material knowledge, regional forms, language, use, and skills passed between generations. When markets mass-produce a “Sami look,” the question is not only imitation, but who may define, make, and earn a livelihood from culture.

## Why it matters

Discussions of Nordic design in Taiwan can easily gather wood, leather, horn, silver, and vivid colours into one image of natural, simple handicraft. Yet `duodji` is not a regional branch of Swedish national aesthetics, nor a style anyone can enter by copying a pattern. Sámi Duodji Sameslöjdstiftelsen describes it as craft and artistic craft made by Sami people and grounded in Sami traditions, ways of thinking about form, patterns, and colours. Different Sami languages also use different corresponding terms.

Since moving from Sweden to Taiwan, I have become more aware of whom the convenient label “Swedish culture” can conceal. Duodji opens that question. Part of Sápmi, an Indigenous cultural region crossing state borders, lies within Sweden. To understand an object, we must put its maker, community knowledge, materials from the land, and market power back into the same frame.

## Not one style, but material and regional knowledge

Duodji can include clothing and dress details, leather and textiles, wood and horn, silverwork, and everyday objects such as vessels, knives, and bags. That may sound like a list of products, but its core is relational: how materials are obtained, whom an object is made for, how forms and techniques pass through families or communities, and how regions retain their differences.

We therefore cannot see a colour or curve and treat it as a universal symbol of all Sami culture. Sameslöjdstiftelsen's terminology page places Northern Sami `duodji`, Southern Sami `vætnoe/duedtie`, Lule Sami `duodje`, and other names side by side. Linguistic variation reminds us that Sápmi is not a pattern library that can be flattened into an “ethnic look.”

The Sami Parliament in Sweden also places duodji within artistic practice, identity, ways of life, and traditional knowledge. That is what separates it from an ordinary product category. The value of a cup, knife, or garment lies not only in the finished object, but in the knowledge its making keeps in use.

## Living heritage needs time, teaching, and income

A tradition existing only in a museum is no longer a complete living capacity. For duodji to continue, people must be able to learn and teach, and makers must have some chance of turning highly skilled, time-intensive work into a livelihood. Sameslöjdstiftelsen supports courses and acts as the Swedish-side trade organisation in journeyman and master-craft certification. Its shop connects customers with nearly 90 small businesses across the Swedish side of Sápmi.

These institutions do not replace transmission within families and communities. They add distribution, support for pricing, and professional recognition needed in a modern market. If a handmade object is compared only with a factory souvenir, the cost of preparing materials, working time, accumulated knowledge, and small-scale sales disappears. Preserving culture therefore includes making sure its practitioners are not forced to prove that culture survives by selling it cheaply.

A language status report from the Sami Parliament further describes duodji as an important carrier of Sami languages and traditional knowledge, while noting the need for stronger language support in knowledge transfer. An object can function as a language environment: when technical terms, material distinctions, and working methods no longer pass between generations, what is lost is not just vocabulary but an entire way of judging the world.

## Certification asks who made it, not only what it resembles

Tourism and design markets reward instantly recognisable patterns, but they also make it easy to separate culture from makers. In its budget material, the Sami Parliament explicitly identifies cultural appropriation, protection of patterns, and loss of traditional knowledge as duodji concerns. Cheap copies do more than take sales. They teach consumers that visual resemblance is the same as cultural legitimacy.

The `Sámi Duodji` and `Sámi Made` certification marks administered by the Saami Council address that information gap. The first identifies duodji made by Sami craftspeople according to traditional materials, techniques, forms, and uses. The second covers a wider range of physical and digital products made by Sami people or Sami-owned businesses. Keeping the marks separate recognises that “Sami-made” and “traditional Sami craft” overlap without meaning the same thing.

This is not the Swedish state certifying Sami culture. The Saami Council states that the marks are owned by the Sami people and administered by the Council; Sámi Duodji Sameslöjdstiftelsen serves as the licensing office on the Swedish side. Their political significance lies in returning cultural definition and market recognition, as far as possible, to Sami institutions and makers.

## From admiring a style to respecting cultural self-determination

For readers in Taiwan, duodji can speak across distance to questions facing Indigenous crafts at home: when a pattern becomes popular, who may use it, who is credited, where revenue goes, and whether tradition may accommodate contemporary creation are all more useful questions than a simple test of “authenticity.”

Respecting duodji does not mean freezing it as an ancient specimen. Contemporary Sami makers create new objects from traditional knowledge, while communities continue to debate boundaries. An outside viewer can begin by identifying makers and certification marks, recognising the labour behind prices, refusing to treat visual elements as ownerless material, and accepting that cultural holders have the right to decide which knowledge may circulate.

This also changes how we look at Swedish art. Sweden is not only a country exporting “Nordic design.” It is also a society that must confront Indigenous cultural self-determination, institutional responsibility, and colonial relationships. What most deserves attention in duodji is not the colour it can add to Sweden's image, but its refusal to let objects be easily consumed after people, land, language, and rights have been removed.
