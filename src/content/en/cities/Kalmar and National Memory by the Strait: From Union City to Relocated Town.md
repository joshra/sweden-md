# Kalmar and National Memory by the Strait: From Union City to Relocated Town

> **30-second overview:** Kalmar stands on Sweden's southeast coast, facing Öland across the Kalmar Strait. The union of 1397 placed it in Nordic political history, but the city's deeper significance lies in how castle, warfare, relocation, and present-day university life occupy the same waterfront landscape.

## Why it matters

For many Taiwanese readers, the name Kalmar first appears in the term "Kalmar Union." That can make the city sound like little more than the backdrop to a political meeting. Kalmar reveals something more important: the Nordic states did not begin with clear, stable borders and only then enter into relations. Monarchy, sea routes, defence, trade, and local towns repeatedly rearranged one another along this coast.

Since moving from Sweden to Taiwan, I have become increasingly interested in how cities keep national history inside ordinary life. Kalmar lacks Stockholm's metropolitan scale, yet it was once called the "Key to the Realm." Its old town was even moved to Kvarnholmen in the seventeenth century. Today, people still work, study, and live among the castle, fortified street plan, railway station, university, and sea. Kalmar is therefore not merely a city that used to matter. It shows how history changes a city's location, form, and understanding of itself.

## A strait that turned an edge into an entrance

Kalmar lies beside the narrow strait between mainland Sweden and Öland. A defensive tower stood on the present castle site by the late twelfth century. The thirteenth-century fortress and town guarded shipping while facing a Danish frontier then located not far to the south. "Key to the Realm" was not simply a tourism slogan. It compressed a geopolitical function: whoever controlled this point could watch the sea route, defend the southeast coast, and absorb pressure from a southern power.

This matters when we are accustomed to reading maps through modern borders. Southern Sweden now appears securely joined into one country, but the relationships among Swedish, Danish, and Norwegian crowns were far more fluid in the medieval and early modern periods. The strait was not the end of national territory. It was where mobility and conflict entered it. Kalmar's importance grew precisely from being close to both water and a frontier.

## The Kalmar Union was not a Nordic unity fairy tale

In 1397, representatives of Denmark, Norway, and Sweden took part in a union settlement at Kalmar, giving the political order led by Margaret I its most famous name. The arrangement placed three kingdoms under a shared monarch, but it did not erase their laws, aristocratic interests, or political conflicts. Cooperation alternated with disputes over power, taxation, appointments, and local autonomy. Gustav Vasa's accession as king of Sweden in 1523 is conventionally treated as the decisive Swedish break from the union order.

Kalmar's most revealing role, then, is not proof that the Nordic region united early. It shows how much negotiation shared institutions require. Looking backwards from today's image of Nordic cooperation can romanticise the union. Yet it was first a dynastic response to security and power. What Kalmar Castle preserves is an ambitious but unstable political experiment conducted before national borders had settled into their modern form.

## After war, the whole town changed location

Kalmar's strategic value also exposed it to war. During the Kalmar War of 1611–13, the town and castle became central to conflict between Sweden and Denmark, and the urban settlement was badly damaged. In the mid-seventeenth century, the state decided to move the town from beside the castle to Kvarnholmen to the north. A major fire in 1647 accelerated that process.

The new town was not an irregular settlement that grew slowly. It was a deliberately planned fortified city. Straight streets, rectangular blocks, a central square, walls, and surrounding water wrote defence and order into its plan. Kalmar municipality's cultural-environment documentation describes Kvarnholmen as one of northern Europe's well-preserved seventeenth-century fortified towns. For Taiwanese readers, this history may feel familiar in another way: relocation and large-scale urban restructuring are never only engineering. They move residents and markets while pressing the state's perception of risk into local life.

The old city did not disappear completely. Gamla stan around the castle and its archaeological layers preserve traces of the earlier settlement, while Kvarnholmen remains today's centre. Kalmar resembles a sheet on which two urban locations are still visible: the medieval port and fortress on one side, and the order redrawn by the early modern state on the other.

## Kalmar still grows inside historic sightlines

As Kalmar's military role declined in the nineteenth century, the city expanded beyond its old defences. The railway opened in 1874, and the Öland Bridge connected the city more directly to the island in 1972. Today Linnæus University occupies Universitetskajen by the harbour, adding students, research, housing, and enterprise to another layer of waterfront development.

That does not make history scenery that can be shifted at will. Kvarnholmen is an area of national cultural-environment interest, and new development must address the relationships among the castle, fortification remains, low skyline, and views across water. Kalmar Castle itself underwent major nineteenth- and twentieth-century restoration and is now managed by the National Property Board. In 2026, it became one of the first cultural properties in Kalmar County marked with the blue-and-white shield for identification and protection under the Hague Convention. Heritage, this reminds us, supports identity in peacetime and is also treated as public infrastructure requiring protection in crisis and war.

Looking at Kalmar from Taiwan, what I want to bring back is not an attractive castle but a city-scale sense of history. The state once attempted union here and later moved the town because of war. New university buildings and neighbourhoods must still find their places inside those inherited sightlines. Kalmar turns Swedish state formation from a chronology into a route one can walk.
