# Sundsvall and the Rebuilding of the Stone City: How Timber Wealth Wrote Fire, Class, and Urban Ambition into the Street Plan

> **30-second overview:** Nineteenth-century timber exports made Sundsvall a major Swedish sawmill centre, bringing labour conflict, fire risk, and extraordinary concentrations of wealth. Stenstaden, the Stone City built after the fire of 1888, was both an exercise in fire-conscious urban planning and a monument through which timber capital inscribed itself into a northern city centre.

## Why it matters

Swedish industrialisation is often introduced through automobiles, telecommunications, or the welfare state that followed. Sundsvall takes us further back, to a raw-material economy. From the mid-nineteenth century, forests, rivers, steam sawmills, ports, and international markets formed one industrial chain here. Modern Sweden was not made inside factories alone; industrialisation also redistributed land, labour, wealth, and urban space.

Living in Taiwan has made me alert to a temptation shared by readers in both places: when a well-preserved quarter looks beautiful, we quickly treat it as an aesthetic achievement. Sundsvall's stone façades deserve attention, but towers, ornament, and broad avenues are only half the story. We also need to ask who could finance rapid rebuilding, who bore the risks of falling timber prices, and who remained outside the monumental streetscape. Sundsvall turns industrialisation from a growth curve into a map of power that can be walked.

## Forests, steam sawmills, and a port facing the world

Sundsvall lies on Sweden's central east coast. Forested river valleys and ports along the Gulf of Bothnia helped make the district a centre of sawmilling in the nineteenth century. Steam power freed mills from a strict dependence on waterfall sites, allowing sawmills and loading facilities to cluster around the bay. Timber travelled from the interior to the coast, was processed, and entered European markets; the local city became tied to global prices and demand.

Growth transformed population and daily life. Workers arrived from across Sweden, while merchants, shipping, and related industries expanded. Sundsvall Museum describes the district's development from a small wooden town into one of Sweden's richest and densest sawmill regions between the late nineteenth and mid-twentieth centuries. Yet “timber brought prosperity” tells only half the truth. Export income and urban consumption grew, but workers' wages, housing, and safety did not improve automatically with them.

## The 1879 strike: power beneath prosperity

When the international economy weakened in 1879 and sawmill owners cut wages, a large strike spread through the Sundsvall district. It took place before national trade-union institutions had matured. Thousands of workers responded to wage reductions through collective stoppage, bringing state authority, local employers, and workers' limited organisational power into direct confrontation.

Sweden's later labour market is often described through negotiation and the organised relationship between unions and employers. Sundsvall reminds us that this order was not born gentle. The Swedish Biographical Lexicon at the National Archives identifies the 1879 strike as one of the early large-scale confrontations between modern large companies and workers demanding better wages and conditions. The municipal museum now places sawmill workers, timber magnates, women, and children within the same industrial history. The city's wealth was never an uncontested common achievement.

## After the 1888 fire, safety and display built a city together

On 25 June 1888, fire destroyed most of central Sundsvall. Municipal material records that 9,000 of roughly 11,000 residents lost their homes. The new plan introduced the broad Esplanaden, firebreaks, and stricter material requirements. Central blocks were rebuilt in stone, brick, metal, and render, giving Stenstaden its name.

If reconstruction is reduced to a story of “rising from the ashes,” it becomes too tidy. The speed and quality were remarkable: around eighty architecturally ambitious buildings appeared in less than a decade, attracting architects and craftspeople. But this urban ambition depended upon private wealth accumulated through the timber boom. Timber merchants and industrialists displayed status through banks, hotels, commercial buildings, and residential façades, while workers' and artisans' environments were not memorialised with equal grandeur.

The Stone City therefore supports two readings at once. It was a public project that embedded knowledge about fire into avenues, materials, and blocks. It was also a project of power through which an industrial elite turned commercial success into the face of the city. These readings do not cancel one another; their coexistence is precisely what makes Sundsvall revealing.

## Preserving relationships, not façades alone

Central Sundsvall is now designated an area of national interest for the cultural environment. The Swedish National Heritage Board's account extends beyond the stone centre built rapidly after the fire. It also includes the seventeenth-century grid, port and railway settings, and workers' and artisans' suburbs alongside the affluent centre. That breadth matters because it refuses to reduce heritage to the most ornate buildings.

The city must, of course, keep changing. Transport, commerce, accessibility, climate adaptation, and city-centre life all place new demands on old streets. Preservation does not mean freezing the 1890s. It means retaining the relationships between forest and port, capital and architecture, labour and politics whenever the city is renewed. Sundsvall suggests that urban resilience is not only the capacity to rebuild more securely after disaster. It is also the capacity to preserve a common memory that does not belong solely to the winners.
