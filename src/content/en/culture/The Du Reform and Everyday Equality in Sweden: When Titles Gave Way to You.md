# The Du Reform and Everyday Equality in Sweden: When Titles Gave Way to You

> **30-second overview:** Swedes today commonly address teachers, managers, and even doctors by first name and use the singular `du`, meaning “you.” Before the mid-twentieth century, politeness often required titles, surnames, and elaborate ways of avoiding a direct “you.” The `du-reformen` was not a reform enacted by government but a rapid social and linguistic shift during the 1960s and 1970s.

## Why it matters

After moving from Sweden to Taiwan, I quickly noticed how much Chinese forms of address can convey at once: age, occupation, seniority, intimacy, and respect. Terms such as teacher, doctor, senior schoolmate, or junior schoolmate position a relationship before the conversation proceeds. Swedish reliance on first names and `du` can therefore look either naturally casual or like proof that everybody has already become equal. Neither interpretation is sufficient.

The deeper importance of the du reform lies in how a society moved hierarchy away from the surface of ordinary sentences. Grammar became simpler, but power in meetings, wage differences at work, and professional authority in medicine did not disappear. `Du` is not equality in the completed tense. It is an everyday expectation that people should be able to speak across different roles without first navigating a maze of titles.

## Before the reform, politeness required verbal detours

In the older Swedish system, directly using `du` with someone unfamiliar or socially senior could seem too intimate. Yet `ni`—the pronoun also used for the plural “you”—was not a reliably safe polite alternative. It could be used downwards in a hierarchy and make the addressee feel placed in an inferior position. Sweden therefore lacked a form that everybody experienced as neutral and respectful.

Speakers relied heavily on titles, surnames, third-person constructions, and sentences that avoided a pronoun altogether. Instead of “Would you like more coffee?”, a host might simply ask whether more coffee was desired. If a position was known, one could ask whether “Director Andersson” had time. Children once addressed unrelated adults as `tant` or `farbror`, aunt or uncle. This was not merely picturesque etiquette. It was a social technology that worked properly only when one knew another person's status, gender, marital position, and occupation.

The system created practical uncertainty. Not knowing the correct title made speaking risky, while selective title use continually restated who stood above whom. A campaign in the 1930s proposed a universal `ni` to replace this complexity. Because the word already carried memories of unequal address, it never supplied the final answer.

## 1967 was a symbol, not an effective date

The `du-reformen` is commonly associated with Bror Rexed. When he became director-general of the Swedish National Board of Health in 1967, he told employees that he wanted them to use `du` with him and hoped to do the same with them. Press coverage turned the gesture into the reform's most memorable scene.

Calling Rexed the inventor of general `du`, however, misses the history. The Institute for Language and Folklore states that the shift was already under way during the 1960s and had begun earlier in parts of society. Urbanisation, expanded education and public services, democratic values less comfortable with visible status differences, and modern workplaces that brought strangers into routine cooperation all made the older system harder to sustain. Rexed publicly articulated a change whose moment had arrived; he did not issue a national command.

The reform consequently had no statute, central programme, or single implementation date. Like many profound changes in daily life, it spread as people copied one another in schools, offices, shops, and media. By the 1970s, first names and `du` had become the broad norm. Institutions did not first order language to change. Society made a different way of speaking feel natural.

## One pronoun rearranged work and public life

Swedish pupils now usually address teachers by first name, and employees commonly do the same with managers. This does not mean classrooms and organisations lack responsibility or authority. It means authority is less often displayed through the form of address. Taiwanese newcomers to Swedish workplaces can easily misread this point: calling a manager by first name does not turn that person into a friend, and `du` does not remove professional boundaries.

At the same time, general `du` lowered the threshold for entering a conversation. When service workers, public authorities, and citizens use the same pronoun, speakers do not first need to judge which title another person deserves. Direct `du` address also became part of Swedish public-sector plain language: addressing readers directly can make administrative writing clearer and easier to act upon.

Linguistic symmetry can nevertheless hide material asymmetry. A doctor and patient both say `du`, but the doctor still possesses specialist knowledge. A manager and new employee use first names, but the manager still evaluates performance. This tension is central to everyday Swedish equality. Status is discouraged from weighing down the greeting, while procedures, labour rules, and professional ethics must still restrain real power.

## Politeness did not disappear when du became normal

Swedes did not discard every form of ceremony. Royal, diplomatic, ritual, or highly formal correspondence can retain special titles. Tone, wording, distance, and situation continue to communicate respect. Politeness moved away from a fixed hierarchy of titles and towards how something is said, when it is said, and whether another person is given room.

Nor did `ni` disappear. The Institute for Language and Folklore notes that some younger speakers use it with older people, and service staff may use it with customers to signal extra respect. Yet people who remember its downward historical use can experience the same word as distancing or even offensive. Address can never be reduced to a mechanical politeness formula.

Seen from Taiwan, the value of the du reform is not to decide which culture is more equal. It is to notice that politeness can be located in different parts of language. Chinese often makes a relationship explicit in the form of address. Modern Swedish tends to begin with a shared “you” and asks context to supply role and respect. One less title does not mean one less boundary. It means people must show in other ways that they understand the boundary.
