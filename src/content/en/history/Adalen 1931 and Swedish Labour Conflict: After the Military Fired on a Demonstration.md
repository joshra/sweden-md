# Adalen 1931 and Swedish Labour Conflict: After the Military Fired on a Demonstration

> **30-second overview:** On 14 May 1931, Swedish soldiers fired on a labour demonstration in Adalen, killing five people. This was not an incidental dark page outside the Swedish model of consensus. It is an entry point into how that model developed: a democratic society learned the terrible cost of treating labour conflict as a military-order problem.

## Why it matters

When Sweden is introduced in Taiwan, labour-market negotiation can sound like national temperament: people are rational and trusting, so they sit down and talk. After moving from Sweden to Taiwan, I have become more convinced that Adalen must be told alongside the later institutions of negotiation. Institutions did not arise because everyone was naturally mild. They arose in part because conflict had escaped control and society learned which boundaries could not be crossed again.

Adalen, along the Angerman River, was an industrial landscape shaped by sawmills, pulp and shipping. In 1931, a local wage dispute, strikebreakers, insufficient policing, military deployment and state responsibility were compressed into a single day. For Taiwanese readers, it offers a practical comparison: democracy is tested not only at elections, but also in how government handles assembly, strikes and public order.

## From wage cuts to troops in the valley

The global economic crisis of the early 1930s intensified unemployment and pressure to reduce wages. Labour conflict around Adalen escalated when employers brought in strikebreakers. Protesters had already removed some of them from their workplace at Sandviken. After local police were judged insufficient, troops were deployed to maintain order and protect those who remained at Lunde.

That decision changed the nature of the conflict. Soldiers trained for combat and carrying live ammunition were inserted into a civil crisis built around wages, a strike and collective action. The issue is not only what individuals on either side did at the scene. The state had first handed a political and labour problem to a tool unsuited to resolving it.

## 14 May: five deaths cannot become only a legend

On 14 May 1931, a demonstration marched towards Lunde. When troops opened fire, Erik Bergstrom, Ernst Eriksson, Sture Larsson, Evert Nygren and Eira Soderberg were killed, and others were wounded. Soderberg was not a worker marching in the demonstration; she was struck nearby. Her death is a reminder that once force escapes control in a civilian setting, danger does not remain inside boundaries drawn by the authorities.

Swedish public memory often recalls the marcher and trumpeter Tore Andersson sounding the military cease-fire, after which the shooting stopped. It is a powerful story, but remembering only a hero in the moment can obscure the institutional question: why did a labour procession need a signal that soldiers recognized in order to stop being shot?

## Responsibility did not settle neatly after the gunfire

The event immediately produced protests across Sweden and fierce parliamentary debate. The government appointed an Adalen commission, while military and judicial proceedings addressed responsibility. Yet the verdicts, sentences and prosecution of demonstrators did not leave a clean conclusion in which the state had fully assumed responsibility. Political camps continued to contest interpretations of provocation, command and self-defence.

That is why Adalen should not be reduced to a single-track morality tale. It is at once a workers' movement memory of martyrdom and a set of legal questions about confusion at the scene, chains of command and the boundary of state violence. Mature historical understanding need not diminish the weight of five lost lives, but it must distinguish evidence, political language and later symbolism.

## From police reform to labour negotiation: a long influence, not one cause

After Adalen, Sweden developed a strongly restrictive tradition around using the military for domestic policing. The state police (`statspolisen`) organization established in 1933 was also directly connected to the shortage of local police revealed in 1931 and the consequences of deploying troops. Even today, Swedish debates about military support to the police cannot avoid the institutional memory of Adalen.

The event is also commonly placed within the longer shift in Swedish labour relations from fierce confrontation towards institutionalized negotiation. But the 1938 Saltsjobaden Agreement should not be presented as the direct result of one volley of gunfire. Party politics, the organizational strength of unions and employers, and the governance of economic crisis formed other paths in between. A more precise claim is that Adalen fixed the cost of uncontrolled confrontation in public memory, while later negotiating institutions gave conflict another outlet.

What I hope Taiwanese readers see here is not a Nordic exception that never shed blood, but a democratic society forced to learn how to design boundaries. The value of consensus is not that it hides conflict. It is that conflict need not return to the line of fire.
