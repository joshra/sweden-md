# Sweden's 1979 Ban on Corporal Punishment: Rewriting Family Authority as Children's Rights

> **30-second overview:** On July 1, 1979, Sweden amended its Children and Parents Code to state that children may not be subjected to corporal punishment or other humiliating treatment. As the first country to enact a comprehensive ban, Sweden did more than prohibit hitting: it recast children as persons whose dignity and bodily boundaries also constrain parental authority.

## Why it matters

When I discuss Swedish ideas about childhood in Taiwan, I often hear a reasonable question: "Does that mean Swedish parents never discipline their children?" The 1979 reform was not a promise that family life would always be gentle. It established a public boundary: being a parent does not grant a special right to hit or humiliate a child.

That distinction matters for Taiwan-facing readers because it moved discipline out of the category of purely private family custom and into the language of children's rights and public responsibility. Sweden did not begin with a ready-made consensus. It took decades of legal revision, professional debate, and public education to place family authority clearly inside legal limits.

## From a parental right to a law that no longer authorised violence

Older Swedish law had recognised room for parents to "correct" children in the course of upbringing. That position weakened during the twentieth century. In 1957, the special criminal-law exemption for minor parental corporal punishment was removed. In 1966, wording in the Children and Parents Code that could support a parental right to use physical correction was also deleted.

But removing legal authorisation was not the same as stating an explicit prohibition. After 1966, uncertainty remained, and some people still believed that lighter physical punishment was lawful discipline. The 1979 amendment closed that gap: children could not be subjected to corporal punishment or other humiliating treatment.

## The 1979 turn: childhood was not an exception to bodily protection

Parliament placed the rule in the Children and Parents Code rather than creating a stand-alone offence aimed only at families. That location matters. It first defined what the parent-child relationship ought to be: children are entitled to care, security, and a good upbringing, and must be treated with respect for their person and individuality.

The ban therefore did not carry its own separate criminal penalty. Conduct amounting to assault or another offence remained governed by criminal law. Its central work was normative and legal: it removed upbringing as a justification for violence and affirmed that children's bodily integrity deserved the same basic protection as adults'.

## A new boundary had to be communicated

The 1978 government bill made clear that changing a statute was not enough. The new rule also had to be explained through media, schools, childcare, parent education, and social support. The target was not only judicial interpretation but everyday assumptions about what adults were entitled to do to children.

This makes the reform recognisably Swedish without making it idyllic. The state declared a value in law, then treated public information and family support as part of implementation. It did not assume that every home would change immediately, or that every struggling family should be understood only through punishment. Clear boundaries, prevention, and support had to operate together.

## From prohibiting violence to building children's rights

Sweden became the first country to comprehensively prohibit corporal punishment of children in 1979. It later ratified the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child in 1990 and incorporated the Convention into Swedish law in 2020. The order is revealing: children's rights did not arrive complete on a single date. They were built through the removal of parental privilege, the recognition of children's dignity, and later systems of oversight and support.

Nor should this history be told as if Sweden eliminated violence in the home. Law can establish a floor, but it cannot by itself remove stress, fear, or unequal power. The durable legacy of 1979 is that "this is only our family's way of disciplining" could no longer end the discussion. A child's bodily boundary had become a boundary protected by public law.
