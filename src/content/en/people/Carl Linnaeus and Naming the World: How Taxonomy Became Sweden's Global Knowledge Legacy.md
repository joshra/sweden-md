# Carl Linnaeus and Naming the World: How Taxonomy Became Sweden's Global Knowledge Legacy

> **30-second overview:** Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778, ennobled as Carl von Linné) established consistent use of the two-part name—genus and specific epithet—for plants and animals, enabling researchers across languages to exchange knowledge through a common vocabulary. Yet this was not an isolated flash of genius: Uppsala's garden, students, correspondence, specimens, and global trade brought the world to his desk, while his classification of humans reminds us that ordering nature has never been an entirely neutral act.

## Why it matters

In Taiwan, birdwatchers use scientific names to distinguish birds with similar common names and changing classifications, while botanists check Latin names alongside local ones. Much of this apparently ordinary shared language took durable form through Linnaeus's eighteenth-century work. For a multilingual island that understands biodiversity through international science, taxonomy is not an antique concern. It still shapes how species surveys, conservation, agriculture, and databases communicate.

Linnaeus also opens a Sweden that predates the twentieth-century welfare state: a knowledge-making country in which a university, learned societies, gardens, patrons, and students helped turn one professor's method into a global standard. But the story cannot be reduced to a Swedish genius naming nature. Knowledge arrived through travel, commerce, and colonial networks; the same classificatory impulse was applied to groups of people. Linnaeus matters precisely because his legacy remains useful and still requires scrutiny.

## What He Changed Was How Science Could Speak Across Languages

Born in Småland in 1707, Linnaeus studied medicine in Lund and Uppsala and became a professor at Uppsala University in 1741. He did not invent the practice of naming a species with two words. His decisive contribution was to use the method consistently and extensively for plants and animals. A genus followed by a specific epithet was easier to record, compare, and circulate than the long, unstable descriptive names common at the time.

*Species Plantarum* (1753) and the tenth edition of *Systema Naturae* (1758) consequently remain historical starting points for modern botanical and zoological nomenclature. This does not mean that biology still accepts all of Linnaeus's arrangements. His “sexual system,” based on numbers of stamens and pistils, was a convenient artificial key that could separate closely related plants. Modern systematics instead reconstructs groups through evolution and common ancestry. What endured most deeply was not every category, but an infrastructure for stable naming and verifiable reference.

## The Uppsala Garden Was a Global Information Exchange

Swedish public memory often pictures Linnaeus bending close to inspect a small flower. The image is not wrong, but it is too solitary. His home and botanical garden in Uppsala served simultaneously as classroom, experimental space, and correspondence hub. Colleagues, students, and family sustained a knowledge-producing household. He led students into the fields around Uppsala and trained the travelling disciples known as his “apostles.”

Seeds, bulbs, animals, and notes flowed back from distant places, and the enterprise was about more than curiosity. Uppsala University's own account notes hopes that Sweden might produce its own tea, silk, pearls, and porcelain; Linnaeus's journeys within Sweden likewise aimed to identify resources that could be exploited and developed. In the Netherlands, he used wealthy merchant George Clifford's globally assembled garden and specimens to produce *Hortus Cliffortianus*. Taxonomy grew alongside trade routes, wealth, imperial collecting, and economic botany. “Discovery” often meant the moment a specimen reached Europe and received a new name, not the moment other societies first knew it.

## The Book Did Not Survive as a Whole, but Names and Specimens Did

The first *Systema Naturae* was a slim work, and successive editions expanded as information arrived. No individual could now gather known life into a single book, but Linnaean names persist across specimens, museums, and digital databases. The Natural History Museum's Linnaean Plant Name Typification Project continues to establish reference material for more than 9,000 plant names published by Linnaeus, enabling researchers to determine what organism a name must denote.

This is the least dramatic and most durable part of his legacy. Names are not attached once and left untouched. Researchers revise groups as evidence changes, preserve relationships between older and newer names, and return to specimens to check identities. For readers in Taiwan, it is more accurate to imagine a public data standard maintained across centuries than “one man naming every living thing.” Linnaeus supplied an extensible grammar; scientific communities have kept correcting its contents.

## Classification Also Made Historical Prejudice Look Natural

The hardest part of this legacy, and one that cannot be omitted, is Linnaeus's classification of humans. He considered humanity one species and did not use today's word “race,” but divided people into four continental and colour-coded “varieties.” In later editions, he attached physical, temperamental, and cultural traits to them, with unequal evaluations of Europeans and Africans. Historians of science at Uppsala therefore make an important distinction: calling him the sole founder of later biological racism is too simple, but excusing him as merely a “child of his time” cannot erase the influence of his categories.

The worldwide authority of Linnaean classification helped give later attempts to naturalise human difference a scientific format and legitimacy. That does not invalidate binomial nomenclature in biology, but it should change how we commemorate Linnaeus. A responsible account need not choose between hero and villain. It can recognise at once that he made knowledge of nature more cumulative across languages and that he placed eighteenth-century hierarchy inside a scientific form that travelled widely. Classification can help us see life; it can also make inherited prejudice appear to be nature itself.
